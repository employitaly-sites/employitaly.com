# Working from Italy temporarily: what employers need to consider

> An employee asks to work from Italy for a while. Here is what employers should check on tax, social security, immigration, and liability before saying yes.

A team member asks to work from Italy for a few months. The request sounds simple. For the employer, it is not.

The employee keeps the same laptop and the same job. But the legal setting changes. Tax, social security, immigration, and employment rules can all be affected by where the work is physically done. This article lists the points an employer should review before approving a temporary stay.

## Why a short stay can create real obligations

Many companies treat remote work abroad as a personal matter for the employee. It is not. The employer is the party that pays wages, reports income, and carries the risk if something is wrong.

A temporary stay can still trigger duties in Italy. Whether it does depends on the facts: how long the person stays, what they do, who they work with, and what authority they have. You should not assume that a short visit is automatically safe.

## Immigration comes first

The right to be in Italy is not the same as the right to work there. A tourist stay does not usually cover remote work for a foreign employer. Some nationalities need a visa or permit. Others can enter freely but may still face rules on work activity.

Before you agree, ask the employee to confirm their nationality, their planned entry route, and the permit they intend to use. Do not rely on a verbal answer. Ask for the document or the official guidance they are following. If the route is unclear, pause the request until it is clarified.

Italy has specific routes for remote workers and for people who relocate for work. Their conditions change over time. Check the current rules with a qualified adviser before you commit.

## Income tax and withholding

An employee who spends enough time in Italy may become tax resident there. Even before that point, Italy may claim the right to tax income earned for work done on its territory.

This matters for payroll. If your home payroll keeps withholding tax as if nothing changed, the employee may face double taxation or a later claim. If Italian tax applies and you did not withhold, the employer can face penalties and back payments.

Tax treaties between Italy and the employee's country may help. They do not remove the need to analyze the case. Get a written tax view before the stay begins, not after.

## Social security

Social security follows its own rules. In many cases the employee stays covered by their home system for a temporary posting. This usually requires proof, such as a certificate, and it depends on agreements between the countries involved.

If no agreement applies, contributions may become due in Italy. That can mean paying twice for the same period. Ask your adviser which rules apply to the employee's home country and request the right documents early. Delays here are common.

## Permanent establishment risk

An employee working from Italy can, in some cases, create a taxable presence for the company. The risk grows if the person signs contracts, negotiates deals, manages local staff, or acts as the face of the company in Italy.

A person doing routine tasks for a short period carries less risk than a sales lead closing deals from a Milan apartment. Define the scope of work in writing. Say clearly what the employee may and may not do while in Italy.

## Employment law and contract terms

Italian labor rules can apply to people who work in Italy, even if the contract is governed by foreign law. Working time, rest periods, leave, and health and safety rules may all matter.

Review the employment contract. Does it allow work from other countries? Does it set limits? A short remote work addendum is useful. It should state the location, the start and end dates, the approved activities, and who pays for extra costs.

## Insurance, equipment, and data

Check that health cover and accident cover work outside the home country. Confirm that company equipment is insured while abroad. Make sure your data security policy allows access from Italy, and that the employee uses secure networks.

## A practical checklist

- Ask for the employee's nationality, dates, and purpose.
- Confirm the right to work or the correct permit.
- Get a written tax opinion for both countries.
- Confirm social security coverage and gather certificates.
- Review permanent establishment risk and limit the scope of work.
- Sign a short remote work addendum.
- Check insurance, equipment, and data rules.
- Set a clear end date and a review point.

## When a temporary stay should become a hire

Some stays start as a few months and quietly turn into a long-term arrangement. If the employee wants to settle in Italy, or the company wants a lasting presence, a temporary workaround stops being enough. At that stage, a proper local employment setup is usually cleaner and safer than stretching a short-term solution.

An Employer of Record (EOR) can employ the person in Italy on your behalf. It handles local payroll, contributions, and compliance, while you direct the daily work. It can be a good fit when you do not have an Italian entity and want to avoid the risks above.

## Bottom line

Saying yes to a temporary stay is a business decision with legal effects. Review it early, document it, and keep the scope narrow. Most problems come from approving the request quickly and asking questions later.
