# Equity and Stock Options for Employees in Italy: A Tax Awareness Guide

> How equity and stock options are taxed for employees in Italy, and what foreign employers should plan for before granting awards to Italian hires.

Foreign companies often want to give their Italian hires the same equity plan used everywhere else. It is a fair wish. It is also where many hiring plans hit trouble. Italy treats equity differently from many other countries. This article gives you the main ideas so you can plan before you grant anything.

This is general awareness, not tax advice. Rules change, and the details depend on the plan and on the person.

## Equity is usually treated as employment income

In Italy, shares or options given because of a job are generally seen as a form of pay. That has a simple consequence. The value an employee receives can be taxed as employment income, not as a capital gain. Employment income is normally taxed at higher rates than investment gains.

The label in your home country does not decide the outcome. A plan called a "share purchase scheme" or "incentive award" can still be treated as pay in Italy. What matters is the link between the benefit and the work.

## The timing question

For any equity award, ask when the tax event happens. There are several possible moments:

- When the award is granted.
- When it vests.
- When an option is exercised.
- When the shares are sold.

In Italy, the taxable moment often falls at exercise or at the point the employee gets the shares, not at grant. But the exact rule depends on how the plan is built. A plan with restrictions, a lock-up or a forfeiture clause may be treated differently from a plain grant.

The risk for you is a tax bill that arrives before the employee has any cash. If the shares are not listed and cannot be sold, the employee may owe tax on value they cannot touch. Good plan design looks at this early.

## Social contributions can apply too

Tax is only half of the picture. Equity treated as pay can also bring social security contributions, for both the employee and the employer. Foreign companies often forget this. They budget for the award and not for the employer cost that follows it.

This matters for your total cost of employment. When you compare the cost of an Italian hire with a hire elsewhere, include the contribution side of any equity you plan to grant.

## Who withholds and reports

Italian payroll works on withholding at source. The employer is expected to withhold tax on taxable pay and report it. When the employer is a foreign company with no Italian entity, this is a real problem. Who calculates the value? Who withholds? Who files?

If the equity comes from a foreign parent and the Italian side is not involved, the employee may be left to report it alone. That leads to errors, late filings and unhappy staff. A better setup has the Italian payroll side informed of every grant, vest and exercise, with data shared on time.

An Employer of Record (EOR) can take the Italian employer role, run payroll and handle reporting. It still needs accurate data from you. It must know the award terms, the dates and the value at each event.

## Valuation is not trivial

For listed shares, value is easy to find. For private company shares, it is not. You need a defensible valuation at the relevant date. Tax authorities can challenge a value that looks too low. Keep records of how you reached it. Use the same method each time.

## Cross-border employees need extra care

If the employee has lived or worked in other countries, more than one tax system may claim part of the benefit. Awards often vest over a long period. During that time the person may move, work remotely from abroad, or become resident elsewhere. Each change can alter who may tax what. Tax treaties help, but you need to apply them with care.

## Alternatives to think about

If a standard stock option plan looks too heavy for Italy, there are other routes:

- Cash-settled awards that track the value of your shares. They are taxed as pay, but they avoid share ownership and valuation issues for the employee.
- A local bonus plan tied to company results.
- Shares offered under a plan that fits Italian rules for employee share ownership.

Each option has its own tax and contribution profile. None is always best. The right choice depends on your company stage, your plan documents and what the employee values.

## A practical checklist

Before you offer equity to an Italian hire:

- Check how the plan will be treated for tax and contributions in Italy.
- Decide who will withhold and report.
- Plan for the case where tax is due and the employee cannot sell.
- Set a valuation method and keep records.
- Review the plan documents for local fit, including language and consent.
- Tell the candidate early what the tax result is likely to be.

The last point is often missed. Candidates compare offers. An award that looks generous on paper can feel poor once tax is clear. Honest talk at the offer stage avoids disputes later.

## Bottom line

Equity can work well in Italy. It needs planning. Treat it as pay, expect contributions, solve the withholding question, and be ready to value shares properly. Do this before the offer letter goes out, not after the first vesting date.
