# Managing Italian Employees in a Global Team: Communication and Time Zones

> How to work well with Italian employees across time zones: set overlap hours, write clear handoffs, respect local rhythms, and keep communication fair.

You hired someone in Italy. The rest of your team is somewhere else. Now the real work starts: making the distance invisible.

Most problems in global teams are not about skill. They are about timing and clarity. This article covers how to set up communication so your Italian employees work well with colleagues in other regions.

## Start with the overlap

Italy sits in a European time zone. If your headquarters is in North America, the overlap is short and falls in the Italian afternoon. If your team is in Asia, the overlap is in the Italian morning. If you are in Europe, there is little to solve.

Do not guess. Map the working hours of every team member and mark the hours when most people are online. Treat that window as shared time. Use it for meetings, quick questions and decisions. Keep everything else asynchronous.

Do not rotate the pain. If one office always takes the early or late call, resentment builds. Share the inconvenience, or move the meeting to the middle of the overlap.

## Respect the local working day

An Italian employee works within local hours. Evening calls with a far-away head office should be rare, not routine. A call that runs late once in a while is fine. A standing meeting at the end of the evening is not.

Italian professionals also value a proper break in the middle of the day. Lunch is a real pause, not a desk meal. Do not book meetings through it. Avoid scheduling in the quiet holiday periods too. Many people in Italy take long breaks in summer and around the end of the year. Ask about planned leave early and put it in a shared calendar.

## Write things down

Asynchronous work only works if the writing is good. Set a few simple rules:

- Every request states the goal, the deadline and the owner.
- Every meeting ends with written notes and actions.
- Decisions live in one place that everyone can find.
- Handoffs at the end of the day say what is done, what is blocked and what is next.

This helps everyone, but it helps non-native English speakers most. Many Italian employees speak excellent English. Still, reading is easier than listening on a fast call. Notes let people check details at their own pace.

## Be direct, but check understanding

Italian business culture can be warm and relationship-driven. People often like to build trust before they dive into tasks. A short personal chat at the start of a call is not wasted time. It is how rapport is built.

At the same time, politeness can hide disagreement. An Italian colleague may soften a concern, or say it is "complicated", when they mean it will not work. Make it easy to be honest. Ask open questions such as "What could go wrong?" or "What would you change?" Follow up in writing so there is no confusion about what was agreed.

If you give feedback, give it in private and with specifics. Public criticism lands badly in most cultures, and Italy is no exception.

## Choose the right channel

Not every message needs a meeting. A simple split works:

- **Chat** for quick, low-stakes questions.
- **Email or a shared document** for decisions and anything that needs a record.
- **Video** for sensitive topics, planning and one-to-ones.

Avoid urgent messages outside local hours. If your tools allow scheduled sending, use it. A message that arrives at the start of the Italian morning is better than one that pings in the middle of the night. Make it clear that people are not expected to answer outside their hours.

## Include Italian staff in the whole team

A remote employee in a small country office can feel far from the centre. Fix this on purpose:

- Invite them to team events and planning, not just task calls.
- Give them visibility with leadership.
- Rotate who presents, so no one office is always the audience.
- Plan an in-person visit now and then. It pays back many times over.

One-to-one meetings matter more when distance is large. Keep them regular and keep them in a time slot that suits the employee, not only the manager.

## Keep the employment side simple

Good communication is easier when the basics are handled. Your Italian employees need a contract, payroll and local compliance that work without you chasing them. An Employer of Record (EOR) takes on the local employer duties, so your managers can focus on the work and the people. Your team gets a clear point of contact for local questions, and you avoid setting up a local entity just to hire.

## A short checklist

- Map the overlap hours and protect them.
- Rotate meeting times fairly.
- Respect lunch, local holidays and leave.
- Write goals, owners and decisions down.
- Ask open questions and confirm agreements in writing.
- Use chat, documents and video for what each does best.
- Include Italian staff in team life, not only in tasks.

None of this is complicated. It takes discipline. Teams that do it well find that location stops mattering.
