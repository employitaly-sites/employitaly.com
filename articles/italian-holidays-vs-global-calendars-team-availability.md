# Italian Holidays vs Global Calendars: Planning Team Availability

> How Italian public holidays and summer shutdowns differ from other calendars, and how to plan cover, deadlines and launches for your team in Italy.

If your company runs on a US, UK or Asian calendar, your Italian team will not be off when you expect. Some of their days off will surprise you. Some of your busiest weeks will be quiet in Italy.

This article shows where the calendars differ and how to plan around it.

## Italy does not follow your calendar

Italy has its own set of national public holidays. Many are tied to the Catholic calendar. Others mark civic events, such as Liberation Day and Republic Day. Some will feel familiar if you come from a Western market. Others will not.

A few differences matter for planning:

- **No Thanksgiving.** US teams often assume a shared late-year lull. In Italy, there is no such pause at that point.
- **Easter Monday is a day off.** Many calendars treat Easter as a Friday and Sunday event. Italy also closes on the Monday after.
- **Ferragosto.** This is a mid-summer public holiday. It sits inside the longest quiet period of the Italian year.
- **Saints' days.** Each city also has a local patron saint day. In some cities, offices close or run light on that day.
- **Bridge days.** When a holiday falls near a weekend, many people take the day between as leave. The office is technically open, but half the team is away.

Your global calendar will not show most of this. You need to add it yourself.

## The summer slowdown

The biggest planning risk is not a single public holiday. It is the summer. Many Italian businesses slow down for weeks in the warmest months. Some close completely. Clients and suppliers go quiet too.

If your Italian employee depends on Italian partners, expect delays in that period. Approvals take longer. Replies are slow. Meetings are hard to book.

This is normal. Italian workers are entitled to rest, and many take their leave in a block. Plan for it the way you plan for year-end in the US.

## The year-end period

The winter break is long in Italy. Many people stay off from before Christmas until after Epiphany, which is a public holiday. If you need your Italian team for a year-end close or a January launch, raise it early. Do not assume they will be at their desks.

## How to plan team availability

Do this once a year and keep it simple.

1. **Build a shared calendar.** Add Italian public holidays next to your home-country holidays. Use a single view for the whole company.
2. **Mark the quiet seasons.** Block the summer and year-end periods as low availability for Italy. Treat them as soft limits for deadlines.
3. **Ask for leave plans early.** Ask your Italian staff to share planned leave well ahead. Many will already know when they want to go.
4. **Set cover rules.** Decide who covers support, payroll approvals and customer calls when someone is away. Write it down.
5. **Avoid launches at the edges.** Do not schedule a release, audit or board deliverable right before a long break. Give the team time to finish and hand over.
6. **Respect local days.** If your employee works in a city with a patron saint day, add it to the calendar. Do not ask for work that day.

## Do not ask people to work through holidays

Some managers from other markets treat holidays as flexible. In Italy, that goes badly. Holidays and rest are part of the employment relationship, and staff expect them to be honoured. Asking for work on a public holiday creates friction and can create legal exposure.

If you need coverage, plan it in advance. Agree it with the employee. Make sure it follows the rules that apply to their contract.

## Time zones are the smaller problem

Teams often worry about time zones first. In practice, holidays cause more missed deadlines. A two-hour gap is easy to manage. A week when half the country is away is harder.

Make holiday awareness part of your onboarding. Show new managers the Italian calendar. Explain why summer and year-end are slow. Teams that know this in advance plan better and push less.
