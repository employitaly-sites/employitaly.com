# How to Harmonise Employee Benefits Across Countries, Including Italy

> Build one global benefits policy that still fits Italy. Learn what to fix centrally, what to localise, and how meal vouchers and welfare fit in.

A global benefits policy sounds simple. Every employee gets the same package, wherever they live. In practice it does not work. Benefits are tied to local tax, social security and market habits. The same package can be generous in one country and strange in another.

The better goal is not identical benefits. It is equal value, set by one policy, delivered in a local way. Think global policy with local fit.

## Start with principles, not products

Do not begin with a list of perks. Begin with what you want the benefits to do. Write down a few clear principles. For example:

- Everyone gets health cover that works in their country.
- Everyone gets a way to save for retirement.
- Everyone gets meaningful time off and family support.
- Total reward stays in a defined range for the same level of role.

These principles travel. A specific perk does not. A gym allowance means little where the real need is private health top-up. A company car is a status item in some markets and a tax problem in others.

## Separate what is fixed from what is local

Split your policy into two parts.

**The global core.** This is what you control from the centre. It covers your principles, your budget logic, your eligibility rules, and your approval process. It also covers how you compare total cost across countries.

**The local layer.** This is what each country decides. It covers the legal minimums, the collective agreements, the tax treatment and the common market practice. In Italy, a lot sits here.

If you try to push the core into the local layer, you create exceptions on every hire. If you push local rules into the core, you create a policy nobody can apply.

## What Italy changes

Italy has strong statutory and collective rules. Many benefits are not optional extras. They come from the national collective agreement that applies to the role and sector. Leave, notice, sick pay and extra payments often depend on it. Your global policy has to sit on top of these rules, not replace them.

This matters in one practical way. Some of your global benefits will already be covered by the local floor. Others will overlap. If you add a global benefit without checking, you may pay twice for the same thing.

Italy also has a developed welfare culture. Employers often give part of the package as welfare instead of cash, because the tax and contribution treatment can be better for both sides. Meal vouchers are the best known example.

## Meal vouchers: a small example of local fit

Meal vouchers are common in Italy. Many employees expect them. Electronic vouchers have a reference figure of 10 EUR per day, a point that came up in recent budget law news. Confirm the current threshold with your local adviser before you set budgets, because thresholds change.

Now look at it from the head office. A US or UK team may see this as a small food perk. For an Italian employee, it is part of normal pay structure. If you remove it or ignore it, the offer looks weaker than the number on paper suggests.

The lesson is general. Do not copy a benefit from your home country into Italy. Ask what the local market uses to deliver the same value, and match that.

## Compare on total cost and total value

To harmonise, you need one way to compare. Use total employer cost on one side and net value to the employee on the other.

For each country, build a simple table:

- Base pay
- Mandatory employer contributions
- Statutory and collective extras
- Voluntary benefits
- Total employer cost

Then look at the employee side. What does the person actually receive, after tax and in useful terms? A benefit that costs you little but is taxed heavily may be worth less than a smaller benefit with better treatment.

This view stops two common mistakes. One is overpaying in countries with high mandatory costs. The other is underpaying in countries where the market expects more through benefits.

## Set guardrails, not rules for every case

Give local managers a range, not a script. For example, define a target band for total reward by role level. Inside the band, let each country choose the mix. Outside the band, require approval.

Also decide in advance:

- Who owns the benefits budget in each country.
- How often you review local practice.
- What happens when a country rule changes.
- How you handle employees who relocate.

The last point is easy to forget. When someone moves to Italy, their benefits do not move with them. The local rules apply from the start. Plan the transition before the move, so the person knows what changes.

## Keep communication simple

Employees do not need to see your whole framework. They need to see what they get, in their own country, in clear words. A short local benefits summary, in the local language, does more than a long global handbook.

Inside the company, explain the logic once. Tell managers that fair does not mean identical. It means the same intent, delivered in the way that works locally.
