# How to Interview Italian Candidates Across Cultures: Format and Expectations

> A practical guide for foreign employers on how to run interviews with Italian candidates: structure, tone, language, salary talk and what candidates expect.

You are hiring in Italy and your interview process was built somewhere else. That is normal. The risk is not that your process is wrong. The risk is that candidates read it differently than you intend.

This guide covers the format that works well in Italy and what candidates tend to expect from you.

## Start with the relationship

Italian interviews are usually less transactional than in many other markets. Candidates want to understand who you are, not only what the job is. Expect a short warm-up before the structured part. Do not rush it.

A friendly but professional opening works best. Introduce yourself, your role and your company. Say clearly why you are hiring in Italy. Candidates often have never heard of a foreign company before, so context matters.

Use formal address in your first emails unless the candidate moves to a casual tone. In English this is less visible, but polite phrasing still helps.

## Choose the interview language on purpose

English skills vary a lot between candidates, even among strong professionals. Decide which language you need for the role and say so in the job post.

If English is required for the work, test it in the interview. If it is not, offer to run part of the conversation in Italian with a local colleague or advisor. A candidate who is nervous in a second language can look weaker than they are.

Send questions in advance if you want deeper answers. Many candidates prepare better this way.

## Expect a walk through the CV

Italian candidates often explain their career as a story. They will talk about their studies, their path and the reasons behind each move. This is not rambling. It is how many people present credibility.

Let them do it, then steer. Ask for specific examples from recent roles. Behavioral questions work, but some candidates are less used to the exact structured format that is common in US or UK interviews. Rephrase if needed. Ask "Tell me about a project you led and what happened" instead of using a rigid formula.

## Multiple rounds are common

Candidates will not be surprised by several stages. They may be surprised by a process with no human contact until an offer. Plan at least a screening call, a conversation with the hiring manager and a technical or practical step where relevant.

Keep the stages short and tell candidates the full process upfront. Silence between rounds is read as lack of interest. Send a quick update even when there is no news.

## Be direct about pay and contract

Salary is a normal topic and candidates want to know early. Give the range for the role and say whether it is gross. Many people think in gross annual terms, but confirm so there is no confusion.

Candidates also care about the full package. Be ready to explain:

- The type of employment contract.
- Working hours and how flexible they are.
- Remote or hybrid options.
- Benefits and any bonus structure.
- Leave and notice terms.

Italian employment is shaped by national collective agreements, and candidates know this. If you can say which one applies to the role, you look prepared. If you are working with an Employer of Record (EOR), explain that the EOR is the legal employer and that day-to-day work happens with your team. Candidates worry about this point more than foreign employers expect.

## Address stability

An unknown foreign company without an Italian entity raises a fair question: is this job safe? Do not wait for the candidate to ask. Explain how the employment is set up, who pays salary and contributions, and how long the company has operated.

Candidates trust clear answers. They distrust vague ones.

## Know the style of the conversation

Italian candidates often speak with energy and will interrupt politely to add a point. This is engagement, not rudeness. Do not read it as lack of discipline.

They may also be modest in a different way. Some will avoid bragging and describe results as team outcomes. Ask "What was your personal part?" to get a clear answer.

On video calls, be punctual, keep your camera on and check the time zone twice. Small signals of respect matter.

## Let candidates interview you

Leave real time for their questions. Good candidates will ask about the team, management style, growth and how decisions are made. They also want to know how a remote manager will support them. Prepare honest answers.

If your company is distributed, say how Italian employees connect with colleagues. Isolation is a common concern.

## Close with clarity

End each interview by stating the next step and when they will hear back. After the final round, move quickly. Strong candidates often have other options, and a slow offer can lose them.

When you make the offer, put it in writing and explain each part. Be ready for a negotiation on salary, start date or flexibility. This is common and not a sign of bad faith.

## Summary

Keep the process human, structured and transparent. Offer the right language. Explain the employer setup and the package. Give feedback fast. Do this and Italian candidates will see a serious employer, even if you have no local office.
