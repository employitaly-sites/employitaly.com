# Life Sciences and Pharma Talent in Italy: Clusters and Roles in Demand

> Where life sciences talent sits in Italy, which roles are hard to fill, and how foreign companies can hire there without opening a local entity.

Italy has a deep life sciences base. It has large manufacturing sites, contract manufacturers, research hospitals and universities with strong science faculties. For a foreign company, the question is not whether the talent exists. The question is where it is, which roles are tight, and how to hire quickly.

This article covers the main clusters and the roles that are hardest to fill.

## Where the talent is

### Lombardy

Milan and the surrounding area form the largest hub. Many multinational pharma subsidiaries have their Italian offices here. So do biotech start-ups, CROs and medical device firms. Several research hospitals and universities feed the pool.

This is the place to look for commercial, medical affairs, regulatory and market access profiles. Competition is high. Candidates in Milan know their value and often hold several offers.

### Lazio

Rome and the area south of it, towards Latina, host a long-standing pharma manufacturing and distribution presence. You find production, quality and supply chain people here. Rome also has public research bodies and hospitals, which supply clinical and scientific profiles.

### Tuscany

Florence and Siena are known for vaccines and biotech. The talent pool is specialised: process development, quality control, and research scientists. The region is smaller than Lombardy, so senior specialists are harder to find, but they tend to stay longer.

### Emilia-Romagna

Bologna, Parma and Modena combine strong universities with a large biomedical and packaging equipment industry. This is a good area for engineers who work at the border of medical devices, automation and manufacturing.

### Veneto, Piedmont and Campania

These regions add smaller clusters. Veneto and Piedmont have manufacturing and diagnostics activity. Campania has a university base and growing biotech and manufacturing interest. Hiring here can mean less competition, but you need to be realistic about the size of the local pool.

## Roles in demand

Some roles are scarce in every cluster.

- **Regulatory affairs.** Italian submissions, labelling and interaction with the national authority need local knowledge. Good regulatory specialists are always in demand.
- **Quality assurance and qualified persons.** Manufacturing and release roles require specific training and experience. Few people hold them, and they do not change jobs often.
- **Pharmacovigilance.** Safety teams need people who know the rules and the tools. The pool is limited.
- **Clinical operations.** Clinical research associates, study coordinators and trial managers are sought by sponsors and CROs alike.
- **Biostatistics and data science.** Italy produces strong quantitative graduates. Many are pulled towards other sectors, so life sciences firms must compete on project interest as well as pay.
- **Process and manufacturing engineers.** Plants need people who can run, validate and improve production. Skilled technicians are especially hard to find.
- **Medical science liaisons and medical affairs.** Doctors and PhDs who can speak with specialists are valued and scarce.
- **Market access and pricing.** Italy has regional health systems, so people who understand how access works across regions are useful.

## What this means for a foreign employer

Three points matter before you start.

**Hire for the cluster, not the country.** A role based in Milan, Siena or Parma draws on a different pool and a different salary expectation. Decide where the person needs to sit. Many roles can be remote, but lab, plant and site-facing jobs cannot.

**Expect long notice periods for senior people.** Senior specialists often have to give extended notice to their current employer. Plan your start dates around this and do not promise an early start you cannot control.

**Get the contract right from day one.** Italian employment is governed by national collective agreements. Pharma and chemical sectors have their own agreements, and these set pay floors, working time, leave and notice rules. They also add benefits that candidates expect. A generic template from another country will not work.

## Hiring without a local entity

Most foreign life sciences companies do not want to set up a subsidiary just to employ a few people. An Employer of Record (EOR) lets you hire in Italy through a local employer. The EOR holds the contract, runs payroll, pays social contributions and applies the right collective agreement. You direct the daily work. You do not need a local legal entity.

This suits several situations:

- a biotech that needs a regulatory lead in Italy before it files
- a CRO that needs clinical staff for a single study
- a manufacturer testing a market before it commits to a site
- a company that needs to hire a candidate who is already in another country and needs a visa and relocation support

If the hire works out, you can later move the team into your own entity. If it does not, you have limited exposure.

## A practical way to start

1. Define the role and the city. Check that the cluster has the profile you need.
2. Find the applicable collective agreement and the likely pay range for that profile.
3. Agree the notice period and start date with the candidate.
4. Set up the contract and payroll through your EOR.
5. Plan onboarding, equipment and compliance training from the first week.

Italy rewards employers who respect local practice and move with clear terms. The talent is there, and it is concentrated in a few places. Know those places and you can hire well.
