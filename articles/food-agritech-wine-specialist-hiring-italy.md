# Hiring Food, Agritech and Wine Specialists in Italy

> How international firms can hire specialist food, agritech and wine talent in Italy, which roles matter, and how an Employer of Record helps you start fast.

Italy is a serious place to build a food, agritech or wine team. The country has deep craft knowledge, strong technical schools and a dense network of producers. For a foreign company, the hard part is not finding people. It is hiring them legally and quickly without setting up a local entity first.

This article covers the specialist roles that matter most, what makes them different from general hires, and how an Employer of Record (EOR) fits in.

## Roles international firms look for

**Enologists and winemakers.** Italian wine is built on regional knowledge. A winemaker who knows Piedmont is not automatically right for Sicily. Soils, grape varieties and local rules differ. When you hire, match the person to the region and the style you want to produce.

**Agronomists.** These specialists advise on crop health, soil, irrigation and yield. They are central to both traditional farms and agritech projects. Many work in the field and travel between sites, so your contract and expense terms should reflect that.

**Food technologists and quality managers.** They handle recipes, shelf life, safety systems and audits. Foreign buyers often need someone on the ground who can speak to Italian producers and keep standards consistent.

**Agritech engineers and data specialists.** Sensors, drones, precision farming software and automation all need people who understand both machines and farmland. This is a smaller talent pool. Expect competition, and expect candidates to value clear contracts and stable terms.

**Export and sales managers.** Italian food and wine brands depend on export. If you are a buyer, importer or distributor abroad, a local sales or sourcing manager can open doors that email never will.

**Sommeliers, brand ambassadors and tasting experts.** These roles support education, events and trade relationships. They are often part-time or seasonal in practice, which affects how the contract is written.

## What makes these hires different

Specialist food and wine roles share a few traits.

First, seasonality. Harvest, pressing and bottling create peaks. Your contract structure should match real work patterns, not just a generic template.

Second, the collective agreement. Italian employment is shaped by sector-level agreements that set minimum pay, hours, leave and notice. Agriculture, food industry and trade each have their own rules. Choosing the wrong one is a common and costly mistake. It affects salary floors, benefits and how your employee is classified.

Third, location. Many of these jobs are rural. Your employee may work from a vineyard, a lab or a farm, not a city office. That changes insurance, travel and equipment questions.

Fourth, certifications and technical qualifications. Some roles need specific training or professional registration. Check this before you make an offer, not after.

## Why not set up a company first

Setting up a legal entity in Italy takes time and ongoing admin. You need a registered office, payroll, tax filings, social security registration and local accounting. If you only need one or two specialists, or you want to test the market, that is a heavy start.

An Employer of Record hires the person on your behalf. You choose the candidate and direct the daily work. The EOR becomes the legal employer in Italy. It runs payroll, handles contributions, issues compliant contracts and manages the paperwork with the authorities.

For food, agritech and wine, this works well in several cases:

- You are a foreign producer opening a sourcing or quality office in Italy.
- You are an agritech company placing a field engineer near your pilot customers.
- You are an importer who wants a local export liaison.
- You are a wine brand that needs a regional specialist before committing to a full entity.

## How to hire well

Keep the process simple.

1. Define the role in practical terms. State where the person works, what they produce and who they report to.
2. Identify the right collective agreement early. It drives pay and conditions.
3. Decide whether the work is full-time, part-time or tied to a season.
4. Agree on how the employee will be equipped, including vehicles, devices and field gear.
5. Plan for language. Many Italian specialists speak English, but technical vocabulary and local suppliers are easier in Italian. A bilingual hire is a strong asset.
6. Write a clear job description and keep reporting lines short.

## What to expect

Italian employees tend to value stability and clarity. A well-structured contract, correct pay and visible benefits matter more than a flashy title. Treat the role as a long-term investment in local knowledge. Producers, growers and buyers in this sector trust people they have known for years.

If your specialist is moving to Italy from abroad, plan early for the right to work, housing and local registration. These steps take time and are easier when handled together with the employment setup.
