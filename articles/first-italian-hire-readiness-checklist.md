# First Italian Hire Readiness Checklist: Decisions Before You Post the Role

> Decisions to make before you post your first role in Italy: contract, collective agreement, payroll, entity route, onboarding and who handles compliance.

Posting a role in Italy is the easy part. The hard part is what happens after a candidate says yes. If you have not made a few decisions first, you will lose the candidate or delay the start date.

Use this checklist before you publish the job.

## 1. Decide how you will employ the person

This is the biggest decision. You have three realistic routes.

- Open an Italian entity and employ directly.
- Use an Employer of Record (EOR) that employs the person on your behalf.
- Engage the person as a contractor.

Contractor status is not a shortcut. If the person works under your direction, on your schedule, as part of your team, Italian rules may treat the relationship as employment anyway. Be honest about how the work will actually be done.

An entity gives you control but takes time and ongoing admin. An EOR lets you hire without an entity. You keep day-to-day management of the work. The EOR carries the legal employer role.

Pick the route first. Everything else depends on it.

## 2. Know which collective agreement applies

Italian employment is shaped by national collective agreements. They set minimum pay levels, working hours, notice periods, leave and other terms for each sector.

You do not choose a salary in a vacuum. The agreement for your sector sets the floor and the job classification. Find out which agreement fits the role before you write a salary range in the advert.

An advert that ignores the agreement will attract offers you cannot legally make.

## 3. Define the role clearly

Write down what the person will do, who they report to and where they will work. This feeds directly into the contract and the classification under the agreement.

Ask yourself:

- Is the role remote, office-based or hybrid?
- Will the person manage others?
- Will they sign contracts or represent your company?
- Do they need to travel?

Vague answers now become disputes later.

## 4. Set the salary with total cost in mind

The salary on the offer is not what the hire costs you. Employer social contributions, insurance and statutory payments sit on top. Italian pay also follows rules about additional monthly payments and accrued leave and severance that many foreign employers do not expect.

Build a full cost view before you commit to a budget. Ask for it in writing from whoever will run payroll. Then decide whether the role still makes sense at that cost.

## 5. Decide who runs payroll and compliance

Someone must register the employee, pay wages on time, file the required reports and handle social security. Italy is strict on all of this, and the paperwork is in Italian.

Choose now:

- Will you build this capability in-house?
- Will you outsource payroll to a local provider and manage the legal side yourself?
- Will an EOR cover both?

If nobody on your team reads Italian employment documents, do not plan to learn on the first hire.

## 6. Check right-to-work early

If your candidate is an EU citizen, the path is simple. If they are not, they may need a visa or permit before they can start. That process takes time and depends on the person and the role.

Do not make a start date promise until you know the candidate's status. Ask in the first conversation, in a neutral and lawful way, whether they have the right to work in Italy.

## 7. Plan the contract and the offer

The offer should match the agreement and the contract type you intend to use. Decide in advance:

- Permanent or fixed term.
- Probation period, if any.
- Notice period.
- Working hours and place of work.
- Benefits you want to add beyond the minimum.

Italian candidates often expect a clear written contract and a clear explanation of the pay structure. A loose offer letter in English will raise questions.

## 8. Prepare onboarding before day one

The new hire needs equipment, system access, a manager and a first-week plan. If you are using an EOR, also agree who handles the HR documents and who answers the employee's questions about pay slips and leave.

A good first week reduces early resignations. It also shows the candidate you are serious about the market.

## 9. Agree how you will exit if it does not work

This is the decision most teams skip. Dismissal in Italy is regulated. You cannot end the contract on short notice for any reason you like.

Understand the rules before you hire. Know the notice requirements and the grounds that are allowed. Decide who would manage a termination. It is much easier to plan this when everyone is happy.

## A quick readiness test

You are ready to post the role when you can answer yes to each of these:

- We have chosen our employment route.
- We know the collective agreement for the role.
- We have a full cost estimate.
- We know who runs payroll and compliance.
- We understand the candidate's right-to-work position.
- We have a contract outline and an onboarding plan.
- We know how termination works.

If you cannot say yes to most of these, pause the advert. A few days of preparation save weeks of delay later.
