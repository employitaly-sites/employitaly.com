# How to Find an Italian Accountant and Advisors as a Founder

> Selection criteria for choosing an Italian accountant, payroll provider and legal advisor when your foreign company starts hiring in Italy.

You are about to hire in Italy. You need local advisors who know how Italian rules work in practice. The wrong choice costs you time, and sometimes penalties. This guide covers how to choose.

## Know which advisors you actually need

Most founders say "accountant" and mean three different roles.

- **Commercialista.** Handles tax, bookkeeping, VAT and company filings.
- **Consulente del lavoro.** Handles payroll, contracts, social security and labor rules. This is the person who matters most when you hire.
- **Avvocato.** A lawyer for corporate matters, disputes, dismissals and complex contracts.

Some firms offer all three. Many do not. Ask early who covers what, and who is accountable for each task.

## Check credentials first

Italian professionals belong to regulated bodies. Ask for the registration of each person who will work on your file. You can verify membership on the public register of the relevant professional body.

A commercialista should be registered with the order of accountants. A consulente del lavoro should be registered with the labor consultants' order. A lawyer should be registered with the local bar.

If someone cannot show registration, move on.

## Look for experience with foreign companies

Italian practice differs from your home country. A good advisor for a local family business may not be right for you.

Ask these questions:

- How many clients do you have that are owned or run from abroad?
- Have you handled a company with no Italian entity, or with a newly formed one?
- Do you have experience with employees reporting to a manager in another country?

Listen for specific examples. Vague answers are a warning sign.

## Test their English

You will read advice, sign documents and make decisions. Poor communication creates risk.

Ask for a short call before you commit. Check three things. Do they explain Italian terms clearly? Do they answer the question you asked? Do they put key points in writing afterward?

Note that official filings and many contracts will be in Italian. Ask who will translate and explain them. Ask whether translations come with the service or cost extra.

## Understand how they price

Fees vary widely. Do not pick on price alone, but do not accept unclear pricing either.

Ask for a written scope that states:

- What is included each month.
- What is billed separately, such as payroll runs, new hires, terminations or tax notices.
- Who pays for government fees and stamps.
- How fee changes are announced.

Fixed fees for recurring work are easier to plan. Hourly rates suit one-off questions. Be careful with very low quotes. They often hide extras.

## Ask about deadlines and process

Italy has strict filing and payment deadlines. Missing one can trigger penalties.

Ask how they track deadlines. Ask what they need from you, and by when. A good advisor gives you a simple calendar and a list of inputs. A weak one chases you at the last minute.

Also ask how they handle a notice from the tax or social security authorities. You want a clear process and a named contact.

## Check systems and access

You should be able to see your own data. Ask whether you get access to a client portal or shared folder. Ask how documents are stored and who can see them.

Ask about data protection. Your employee data falls under EU rules, so the firm must handle it correctly. A serious firm will answer without hesitation.

## Match size to your stage

A solo professional is often responsive and affordable. The risk is dependency on one person. If they are ill or leave, who covers?

A larger firm has depth and backup. The risk is that your small account gets junior attention.

For a first hire, a small or mid-sized firm with a named senior contact often works well. Ask who will actually do the work day to day.

## Ask for references

Request two or three clients similar to you. Speak to them. Ask about response time, accuracy and how problems were handled.

Do not rely only on online reviews. Many professionals get clients by referral, so reviews are thin.

## Plan for coordination

Your accountant, payroll provider and lawyer must share information. Ask whether they already work with the other advisors you plan to use. Decide who is the main point of contact. Without this, tasks fall between roles.

## Consider whether you need to build this network at all

Setting up advisors takes time. You must vet them, manage them and pay several parties. If you only want to hire a few people, an Employer of Record can cover payroll, contracts and compliance under one agreement. You still may need a tax advisor for your wider business, but the employment side is handled for you.

## A short checklist

1. Define which roles you need.
2. Verify registration for each professional.
3. Confirm experience with foreign-owned companies.
4. Test communication in a trial call.
5. Get a written scope and fee list.
6. Review their deadline and notice process.
7. Check data access and protection.
8. Speak to references.
9. Agree on one main contact.

Take your time on this choice. A good advisor saves you more than they cost.
