# How to Set a Competitive Salary Band in Italy

> Build a salary band in Italy by starting from the CCNL floor for the role, then adjusting with market data, total cost and your internal pay structure.

A salary band in Italy starts from a legal and contractual floor, not from a market survey. If you begin with market data alone, you may land below the minimum for the role. If you begin with the floor alone, you may not attract anyone.

This guide shows how to combine both.

## Start with the CCNL

Most employees in Italy are covered by a national collective labour agreement, known as a CCNL. It sets minimum pay by job level and sector. Employers must respect it, even when the employment contract says something different.

The CCNL does more than set a base figure. It also defines how pay is structured, what extra payments apply, how overtime is treated, and how much paid leave and notice the employee gets. These terms change the real cost and the real value of an offer.

So the first step is simple. Identify which CCNL applies to the role, then find the level that matches the duties. The floor is the minimum for that level. Your band cannot start below it.

## Choose the right agreement and level

The agreement follows the activity of the employer, not the nationality of the parent company. A software firm, a logistics business and a consulting company can each fall under a different CCNL. Pick the one that fits the real activity in Italy.

Level matters as much as sector. Each level describes a set of tasks and a degree of autonomy. A job title from your home market may not map cleanly. A "senior manager" in your org chart could sit at a different level here. Map by duties, not by title.

Getting this wrong has two costs. If you classify too low, you risk disputes and back payments. If you classify too high, you pay more than you need to for the whole term of the contract.

## Add market data above the floor

The CCNL floor tells you the minimum. Market data tells you what it takes to hire.

For most skilled roles, the market pays above the contractual minimum. This is common in technology, finance and other competitive fields. In lower-demand roles, the market may sit close to the floor.

Use several sources for market data:

- Salary surveys from recognised providers in Italy.
- Job postings for similar roles in the same city.
- Feedback from recruiters who place candidates locally.
- Your own offers and rejections from earlier searches.

No single source is enough. Compare them, and note the date of each. Pay levels shift, and old data misleads.

Location also matters. Pay in a large northern city often differs from pay in a smaller town or in the south. If the role can be done remotely, decide whether you will pay by location or at one rate for the country.

## Build the band

Once you have the floor and the market range, set three points.

**Minimum.** Never below the CCNL floor for the level. For a competitive offer, set it a little above.

**Midpoint.** The market rate for a fully competent person in the role. This is where most hires should land.

**Maximum.** The ceiling for top performers or people with rare skills. Keep room for growth so you do not have to promote someone just to give a raise.

Keep the band narrow enough to be meaningful. A very wide band makes offers hard to defend and invites unequal pay for similar work.

## Look at the whole package

Candidates in Italy compare more than the base figure. They look at the full package. That includes the extra monthly payments set by the CCNL, the leave allowance, supplementary health cover, meal vouchers, and any variable pay.

Some of these items are required by the CCNL. Others are optional, and they can help you compete without raising the base. Meal vouchers and health plans are common tools. Variable pay can reward results while keeping fixed cost under control.

Be clear about which items are gross and which are net. Candidates often think in net terms, while employers budget in gross terms. A good offer explains both.

## Remember total employer cost

The salary is not the cost. Employer social contributions, severance accrual, insurance and other charges sit on top. Budget the total cost for each point in your band, not just the pay figure.

This step prevents surprises. A band that looks affordable in gross pay can exceed budget once contributions are added. Run the numbers at the minimum, midpoint and maximum before you publish the band.

## Check internal fairness

Compare the band with your pay structure in other countries. You do not need identical pay. You do need a reason for each difference, and the reason should be based on role, level and local market, not on guesswork.

Document how you set the band. If a question comes up later, you can show the CCNL level, the data you used and the date you reviewed it.

## Review on a schedule

A band is not permanent. CCNLs are renewed, and minimums change when they are. Market pay moves too. Set a regular review, and check the band again whenever a new agreement comes into force.

## A short checklist

- Identify the CCNL that applies to the activity.
- Match each role to a level by duties.
- Record the contractual floor for each level.
- Collect market data from several sources and note the dates.
- Set a minimum, midpoint and maximum.
- Add the package items that help you compete.
- Calculate total employer cost at each point.
- Review the band on a fixed schedule.

This approach keeps you compliant and competitive. The floor protects you from underpaying. The market data protects you from losing candidates.
