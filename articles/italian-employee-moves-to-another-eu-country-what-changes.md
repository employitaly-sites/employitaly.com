# An Italian Employee Moves to Another EU Country: What Changes for the Employer

> An Italian employee relocates within the EU. See what changes for payroll, social security, tax and the employment contract, and what stays the same.

An employee you hired in Italy tells you they want to move to another EU country. They want to keep the job. What changes for you?

The short answer: more than most employers expect, and less than they fear. The answer depends on one question. Will the person work from the new country, or will they only live there for a short time while still working in Italy?

## Start with the type of move

There are three common cases.

**A temporary posting.** The employee works abroad for a limited period and then returns. The Italian employment relationship stays in place.

**A permanent move with the same job.** The employee settles in the new country and works from there, often remotely. Your Italian base is no longer where the work happens.

**A transfer to a local role.** The employee leaves the Italian contract behind and signs a new one under local rules.

Each case triggers different rules. Treat them separately.

## Social security

Within the EU, one country's social security system normally applies to a worker at a time. EU coordination rules decide which one. In most cases it is the country where the person actually works.

For a temporary posting, the employee can usually stay in the Italian system. You need to obtain the proper certificate from the Italian authorities to prove it. Without it, the host country may ask for its own contributions.

For a permanent move, the host country's system usually takes over. That means registering the employer there, or using a local representative who can do it for you. Italian contributions stop. Local ones start. The rates, the bases and the benefits are different.

Do not assume the old setup carries over. Check before the first payslip is issued abroad.

## Income tax and withholding

Tax follows residence and place of work. A person who moves their home to another country usually becomes a tax resident there. The Italian withholding obligations may end. New withholding duties may begin in the host country.

If the employee works in the new country, you may have to run payroll there. Or you may need a local entity or provider to do it. Employers sometimes miss this step. They keep paying the person through Italian payroll and create a compliance gap.

There is also a risk of double taxation. Tax treaties between countries reduce it, but someone has to apply them correctly.

## The employment contract

The Italian contract does not vanish when the employee crosses a border. But the rules that govern it can change.

Two points matter most.

First, mandatory protections of the country where the person works can apply on top of the contract. These cover working time, rest periods, minimum notice and similar items. The contract may say Italian law applies. Local mandatory rules can still override it.

Second, the Italian national collective agreement tied to the role may no longer fit. The host country may have its own sector rules. Terms such as leave, notice and pay structure can differ. Review the contract and decide whether to amend it or replace it.

## Permanent establishment risk

A foreign company with an employee working in a country can create a taxable presence there. Authorities may argue the company has a permanent establishment. This can bring corporate tax and registration duties.

The risk is higher when the employee signs contracts, manages clients or acts as a senior decision maker. It is lower for support roles. But it exists in every case. Get advice before the move, not after.

## Practical steps for the employer

Use a short checklist.

- Confirm the type of move and its expected length.
- Identify which country's social security applies.
- Obtain the right certificate if the employee stays in the Italian system.
- Register in the host country if local payroll is needed.
- Review tax withholding in both countries.
- Check mandatory local employment rules.
- Update the contract or sign a new one.
- Assess permanent establishment exposure.
- Tell the employee in writing what changes and when.

## What does not change

The person is still your employee. Their right to live and work in another EU country is not in question. EU citizens do not need a work visa to take a job there. The paperwork is about your obligations as employer, not their right to move.

## Where an Employer of Record helps

A company with no entity in the host country faces a choice. It can set one up, or it can use an Employer of Record (EOR). An EOR becomes the legal employer locally. It runs payroll, registers with the social security system and applies local employment rules. You keep directing the work.

This is often faster than opening a branch for one or two people. It also lowers the permanent establishment risk, since the EOR carries the employment obligations.

Before any move, speak to someone who knows both countries. A small error in the first month can turn into a large correction later.
