# Can a Foreign Company Employ Someone in Italy Directly?

> Can a company with no Italian presence hire in Italy? Plain answers on your options: direct hire, your own entity, a contractor, or an Employer of Record.

## The short answer

Yes, but not in the way most people hope. A foreign company can employ someone who lives in Italy. What it cannot do is skip the local setup that comes with being an employer there.

If you hire someone in Italy, Italian rules apply to that person's job. It does not matter where your head office is. The employee works in Italy, so the contract, pay slips, taxes and social security follow Italian practice.

That leaves you with a few realistic ways to do it. Here they are in plain terms.

## Option 1: Hire directly, with no local company

This is what people usually mean by "direct employment". Your foreign company signs the contract and pays the person itself.

It is possible in principle. In practice it is hard. You still need to:

- register as an employer with the Italian authorities
- run payroll that follows Italian rules
- pay employer and employee social contributions
- withhold income tax from the salary
- follow the national collective agreement that applies to the role
- provide the benefits and leave that Italian employees expect

You need local knowledge for each of these. Most foreign companies do not have it in-house. Mistakes are common, and the employee, not only you, feels the effects.

There is also a tax question. Having an employee working for you in Italy can lead the Italian tax authorities to say your company has a taxable presence there. This depends on what the person does. Ask a local adviser before you assume it will not apply to you.

Direct hiring can work if you already have Italian advisers and a clear plan. For a first hire, it is rarely the easy path.

## Option 2: Open your own Italian company or branch

You can set up a local subsidiary or a branch. That gives you a proper home for Italian employees.

The upside is full control. The employees work for a company that exists in Italy. You build your own presence in the market.

The downside is effort. You need to set up the entity, open a bank account, appoint someone to run it, and keep accounts and filings in order. You also pay for accountants and payroll providers on an ongoing basis. If you only want to hire one or a few people, this can be a lot of overhead. It makes more sense when you plan a real, lasting business in Italy.

## Option 3: Treat the person as a contractor

Some companies ask the person to invoice them as a freelancer. It looks simple. It is often the riskiest choice.

Italian rules look at how the work is really done, not at what the contract is called. If the person works set hours, follows your instructions, and works only for you, the arrangement may be treated as employment anyway. That can mean back payments and penalties.

Contractors make sense for real independent work: a project, a specialist, someone with several clients. They are not a shortcut for a full-time team member.

## Option 4: Use an Employer of Record (EOR)

An Employer of Record is a local company that becomes the legal employer of your person in Italy. You choose the person and decide the work. The EOR handles the paperwork around it.

In practice, the EOR:

- issues a compliant Italian employment contract
- runs payroll and pays the person on time
- pays taxes and social contributions
- keeps up with the right collective agreement
- deals with the authorities
- can help with visas and relocation if the person is coming from abroad

You pay the EOR a service fee on top of the employment costs. In return, you do not need your own Italian entity.

This is the fastest route for a first hire or a small team. It also works well when you want to test the market before committing to a company of your own. If you later set up a subsidiary, the employees can usually move across to it.

The trade-off is that the EOR is the legal employer. You manage the day-to-day work, but contract changes and dismissals go through the EOR and must follow Italian rules.

## How to choose

A few questions help:

- **How many people will you hire?** One or a few points to an EOR. A large team points to your own entity.
- **How long do you plan to stay?** A short test points to an EOR. A long-term base points to a subsidiary.
- **Do you have Italian advisers already?** If yes, direct hiring or your own entity gets easier.
- **Does the person really work independently?** Only then is a contractor arrangement safe.

Whatever you pick, get the setup right from the start. Fixing a wrong arrangement later costs more than doing it properly now.

## Bottom line

You can employ someone in Italy without being Italian. But the employment has to run through an Italian-compliant setup. For most foreign companies hiring their first person, an Employer of Record is the simplest way to do that.
