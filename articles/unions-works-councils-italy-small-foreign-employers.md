# Unions and works councils in Italy: what small foreign employers actually meet

> A realistic look at unions and works councils for small foreign teams hiring in Italy: what they are, when they show up, and what to do about them.

Many foreign founders hear "Italian unions" and picture strikes and long negotiations. For a small team, the real picture is much quieter. Unions matter in Italy, but mostly through paperwork and rules, not through confrontation.

## Where unions sit in the system

Italian unions work mainly at sector level. They negotiate national collective agreements, known as CCNL, with employer associations. These agreements set minimum pay, working hours, leave, notice periods and many other terms for a whole industry.

This is the part that reaches you. Your employee will almost certainly fall under one CCNL. You do not negotiate it. You apply it.

So the first practical contact with the union world is not a meeting. It is a choice of agreement and a payroll set up to follow it.

## Picking the right collective agreement

The CCNL depends on what your company does and on the role of the person you hire. A software team, a sales office and a small consulting unit may each map to a different agreement.

Getting this wrong is the most common mistake. It can lead to wrong pay levels, wrong leave, and disputes later. The employee may notice the difference. Their union rep, if they have one, will certainly notice.

Check the agreement before you write the contract. Keep a note of which agreement applies and why.

## Works councils: what they are and when they appear

Inside a workplace, employees can form union representation. In Italy this usually means a company union representation body, such as an RSA or an RSU. These bodies speak for workers at company level, on top of the sector union.

For a small team, this is rare. Representation bodies tend to form where there are enough employees to want one and where a union has a presence on site. A foreign company with a few people in Italy will usually never see one.

That said, the right to organise exists. Employees can join a union, hold meetings and ask for information. You cannot discourage this or treat people worse for it. Plan for it as a normal part of the legal landscape, not as a threat.

## What small employers really encounter

In practice, small foreign employers meet unions in a few ordinary ways.

**Through the CCNL.** Pay scales, job grades, leave and notice all come from the agreement. Most of your day-to-day contact is reading it correctly.

**Through a departing employee.** When someone leaves or a dispute arises, a union representative may help the employee check their final pay and contract terms. This is routine. A clean payroll file makes it a short conversation.

**Through labour office procedures.** Some employment steps are handled with union assistance, especially around settlements. Your adviser should guide you through those.

**Through a worker who is a member.** Membership alone changes little for you. The union may collect dues through payroll if the employee asks. That is an admin task.

## What does not usually happen

Small teams rarely face collective bargaining at company level. They rarely face industrial action, because a strike needs a workforce and a dispute to act on. They rarely face long consultation rituals.

The risk for a small team is not conflict. It is small errors that pile up: the wrong agreement, a missed allowance, a contract term that conflicts with the CCNL. These are quiet problems, and they surface at the worst moment, such as when a person leaves.

## How to keep it simple

- Confirm the correct CCNL before hiring, not after.
- Draft the contract so it matches the agreement, including grade and pay components.
- Run payroll strictly by the agreement's rules.
- Keep records of hours, leave and payments in order.
- Answer employee questions about their agreement openly. Do not treat them as hostile.
- If a union representative contacts you, respond promptly and in writing, and involve your local adviser.

## The role of an Employer of Record

If you hire through an Employer of Record (EOR), the EOR is the legal employer in Italy. It applies the right collective agreement, runs payroll to its rules and handles contact with unions and labour offices. You manage the person's daily work. The EOR manages the compliance.

For a small team, this removes most of the learning curve. You do not need to become an expert in Italian industrial relations to hire one or a few people well.

## A fair summary

Unions are part of the Italian system, and the collective agreement will shape your employee's terms. But for a small foreign employer, the reality is administrative, not adversarial. Choose the right agreement, follow it, keep clean records, and treat employees' rights as normal. That covers nearly everything you are likely to meet.
