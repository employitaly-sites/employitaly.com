# Onboarding in Italy: first-week practices new hires remember

> How to run the first week for a new hire in Italy: contract papers, a named buddy, face time with the team and a clear plan that people remember.

A new hire in Italy forms an opinion about your company very fast. The first week decides whether they feel welcome or feel like a file number. This is true everywhere, but a few local habits make it more pronounced in Italy.

This article covers what to prepare, what to do on the first day, and what to keep going through the rest of the week.

## Start before the first day

Italian employees often wait for the start date with some uncertainty. Many have left a stable job. Many have a notice period to serve, and some have family who ask how the new employer is.

Silence in this period hurts. Send a short, personal message from the future manager. Confirm the start date, the place or the remote setup, and who will meet them. Keep it human. A line about what they will work on first is enough.

Also send the practical items early:

- The signed contract, in Italian, with an English copy if the working language is English.
- The list of documents you need from them, such as tax code, bank details and identity papers.
- Information on how payroll and benefits work.

If your company hires through an Employer of Record (EOR), explain this clearly. The employee will have a contract with the local employer and will work day to day for you. Say who handles what: payroll questions go to one place, daily work goes to the manager. Confusion about this is a common cause of first-week frustration.

## Make the first day personal

Italian workplace culture values relationships. People want to know who they work with before they dive into tasks.

On the first day:

- Have the manager greet them live, by video if remote.
- Introduce the team one by one, with a sentence about what each person does.
- Assign a buddy, a peer who answers small questions without judgment.
- Share lunch or a coffee, even a virtual one. A shared coffee is not a small thing in Italy.

Do not fill the day with forms and compliance slides. Do the paperwork, but keep it short and separate from the welcome.

## Equipment and access must work

Nothing damages trust like a laptop that arrives late. Check that the hardware is delivered before the start date. Test accounts, email, chat and any tools. If something will be late, tell the person ahead of time and give a date.

For remote staff, send a small welcome item from the company if you can. It does not need to be expensive. It shows that someone thought about them.

## Give a clear plan for the week

Italian professionals dislike vague starts. They accept that learning takes time, but they want to see a structure.

Share a simple plan with:

- What they will learn each day.
- Who they will meet.
- What you expect by the end of the week.
- When the manager will check in.

Keep the targets modest. A first small deliverable by the end of the week gives a sense of progress. It should be something they can finish and be proud of.

## Explain the company, not only the role

Foreign companies often assume that the Italian hire already knows the culture of the head office. They do not. Spend time on how decisions are made, which hours people are online, how feedback works and what the mission is.

Be honest about time zones and meeting load. If the team works across several regions, explain how that affects the Italian workday. Respect for working hours and for time off matters to many employees in Italy. Showing that you understand this early builds credibility.

Also explain the benefits they have in Italy in plain terms. Payslips can look complex. A short walk-through of the payslip with someone from the payroll side prevents questions and worry later.

## Check in often and listen

The manager should speak with the new hire every day in the first week, even if only for a few minutes. Ask what is unclear. Ask what was good and what was not.

At the end of the week, hold a longer conversation. Cover what went well, what is missing and what will happen next. Write down any problem and act on it. People remember when their feedback leads to a change.

## Avoid common mistakes

- Leaving the new hire alone with a pile of documents.
- Starting without a manager present.
- Mixing up who is the employer and who is the manager.
- Late equipment or missing access.
- No contact between signing and start date.

Each of these is easy to prevent with a checklist and one person who owns the process.

## What people remember

Employees rarely remember the slide deck. They remember being greeted by name, having a buddy, working equipment, a clear plan and a manager who listened. These are low-cost habits. They depend on preparation, not on budget.

If you do them well, the first week sets a tone of trust. That tone carries into retention, performance and how your new hire speaks about you to others in their network.
