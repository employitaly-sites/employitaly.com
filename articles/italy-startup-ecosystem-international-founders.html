Italy's Startup Ecosystem for International Founders: Hubs, Accelerators and Investors

Italy is not Silicon Valley. It does not try to be. But it has a startup scene that is real, spread across several cities, and open to founders from abroad. If you plan to build a team in Italy, it helps to know where the activity is and who funds it.

This guide covers the main hubs, the types of accelerators, and the investor landscape. It also explains how to hire people there before you set up a company.

Hubs  

Italian startup activity is spread out. There is no single center. Each city has its own strengths.

Milan is the business and finance capital. Many venture funds, corporate innovation teams and professional services firms are based there. Founders who want investor access often start in Milan.

Turin has an industrial past and a strong engineering base. Its technical universities feed talent into software, mobility and manufacturing technology.

Rome has a large public sector and a growing technology community. It suits founders who sell to government bodies or large institutions.

Bologna combines strong universities with a dense network of manufacturers. It works well for deep tech, robotics and industrial software.

Naples, Padua, Trento and other cities also host university spin-offs and research groups. Smaller hubs can offer lower costs and less competition for talent.

The practical point is simple. Pick the city that matches your sector and your hires, not the one with the biggest name.

Accelerators and incubators  

Italy has several kinds of programs for early-stage companies.

  • University incubators. Many technical universities run programs for spin-offs and student teams. They offer space, mentors and access to research labs.
  • Corporate accelerators. Large Italian companies in energy, banking, fashion, food and manufacturing run programs that pair startups with business units. The upside is a pilot customer. The downside is a slow decision process.
  • Independent accelerators. Private programs offer small investments in exchange for equity, plus mentoring and demo days.
  • Public and regional programs. National and regional bodies support innovative companies with services, grants and incentives. Terms change often, so check current calls before you plan around them.

For an international founder, the most useful programs are those that accept teams from outside Italy and run in English. Ask each program about language, residency requirements and equity terms before you apply.

Investors  

The Italian investor base includes angel networks, early-stage venture funds, corporate venture arms and public-backed investment vehicles. Foreign funds also invest in Italian companies, often alongside local partners.

A few observations help set expectations.

  • Early-stage rounds are common. Later-stage capital is thinner, so many companies look abroad for larger rounds.
  • Angel groups matter. They often lead first rounds and open doors to funds.
  • Corporate investors bring customers as well as capital. That can be worth more than the cheque.
  • Public-backed funds can co-invest with private partners and add credibility.

If you already have investors abroad, an Italian co-investor can help with local introductions and trust. If you are raising locally, expect investors to ask about your plan for hiring and your plan for retaining talent.

Talent  

Italy produces strong engineers, designers and scientists. Many are open to working for foreign companies, especially remotely or in hybrid roles. Competition for senior people exists, but it is lower than in the largest tech markets.

Salaries, notice periods and employment terms follow Italian rules and national collective agreements. These are different from what many foreign founders expect. Getting them wrong early creates cost and risk later.

Hiring before you have a company  

Many international founders want to test the market first. They want to hire a few people in Italy without registering a local subsidiary.

An Employer of Record (EOR) handles this. The EOR becomes the legal employer in Italy. It runs payroll, social contributions and compliance. You manage the daily work. Your team gets a compliant Italian contract and you avoid the setup time of a new entity.

This fits startups well. You can start with one hire, add more as you grow, and convert to your own entity when the numbers justify it. An EOR can also support visas and relocation for people who move to Italy from abroad.

How to start  

  1. Choose the hub that matches your sector and the talent you need.
  2. Shortlist programs and investors that work in English and accept foreign teams.
  3. Confirm current incentives and rules directly with the program or authority.
  4. Hire your first people through an EOR while you validate the market.

Italy rewards founders who arrive with a clear plan and local help.
