Italy's tax wedge relief: why net pay is higher than expected, and what it means for your offers

Many foreign employers build an Italian offer the same way they build one at home. They take a gross salary, apply a rough tax rate, and guess the net. Then the candidate’s own calculation comes out higher. The gap is usually the tax wedge relief.

This article explains what the relief does and how it changes the way you should make offers.

What the tax wedge relief is  

The tax wedge is the difference between what an employer pays and what an employee takes home. Italy has added several measures that cut the wedge for employees on lower and middle incomes. They work on the employee’s side of the payslip. They reduce income tax, so net pay rises without a change in the gross figure.

There are three parts to know.

1. A share of employment income is not taxed  

For lower incomes, a fixed share of employment income is simply excluded from tax. The share depends on the income band:

  • Up to 8,500 EUR: 7.1% of employment income.
  • From 8,500 to 15,000 EUR: 5.3%.
  • From 15,000 to 20,000 EUR: 4.8%.

The share falls as income rises. The relief is largest, in relative terms, for the lowest paid roles.

2. An extra tax deduction in the middle range  

For incomes between 20,000 and 32,000 EUR, there is an additional deduction of 1,000 EUR per year. Above 32,000 EUR it shrinks. It reaches zero at 40,000 EUR.

This matters because many first hires for a foreign company sit in this range. Junior and mid-level staff, support roles, and sales or operations people often fall here.

3. A supplementary payment for the lowest incomes  

There is also an integrative treatment of up to 1,200 EUR per year. It applies to incomes up to 15,000 EUR and only under certain conditions. It is less likely to affect a typical offer, but it shows the direction of the system.

Why net pay looks higher than expected  

Most online salary tools and old rules of thumb do not model these measures well. A candidate who runs a current payroll calculation sees a net figure that is higher than the one your estimate gave.

This helps you in two ways.

First, you may not need to raise the gross salary to hit a net target. If a candidate says they need a certain take-home amount, check it against a current calculation before you add to the gross.

Second, your offer may look more attractive than you expected. A gross figure that looks modest on paper can produce a competitive net figure.

What this means for how you build an offer  

Start from the net the candidate cares about. Italian candidates often think in monthly net pay. Work out the gross that gives that net, using the current rules. Do not copy a gross from another country.

Check where the salary sits relative to the bands. The relief changes shape as income rises. A small raise in gross can move a candidate out of a band where a larger share of income is untaxed. The net gain from the raise can be smaller than it looks. Look at the net result of each option before you decide.

Be careful near the upper edge of the range. The 1,000 EUR deduction phases out between 32,000 and 40,000 EUR. In that stretch, each extra euro of gross brings less extra net than it does elsewhere. If you are choosing between two salary levels in that zone, compare them on net.

Do not promise a net figure from a rough estimate. The rules depend on the income band and on personal conditions. A calculation based on the actual contract and the employee’s situation is the only reliable base.

Keep the employer cost separate. The relief changes the employee’s tax. It does not change what you pay in employer contributions. So a better net for the employee does not mean a lower cost for you. Budget the full employer cost on its own line.

A note on the figures  

The figures above come from secondary sources and describe how the measures work. Rules and thresholds can change, and the final result depends on the individual’s situation. Always confirm with a payroll calculation on the actual contract before you send an offer.

The practical takeaway  

Italian net pay is often higher than foreign employers expect. That is a result of deliberate tax relief for employees, not an error in your numbers. Use it. Build offers from the net backwards, test each salary level against the bands, and avoid raising gross pay when the net is already competitive.

A clear and accurate net figure also builds trust early. Candidates see that you understand local pay, and that helps you close the hire.
