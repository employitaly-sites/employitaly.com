# When to Move From an EOR to Your Own Italian Entity

> An EOR is the fastest way to hire in Italy. Here are the signals that tell you it is time to set up your own Italian entity, and how to plan the move.

An Employer of Record (EOR) is a sensible way to start hiring in Italy. You get people on payroll quickly. You do not need to register a company first. You test the market before you commit.

But an EOR is not meant to be permanent for every company. At some point, owning the entity can make more sense. This article lists the signals that tell you when.

There is no single magic headcount. The decision depends on your plans, not on a fixed number. Look at the signals below and judge them together.

## Signal: the team is growing and stays

An EOR works well for a small team or a pilot. If you keep adding people and plan to keep adding more, the picture changes. The service fee is paid on every employee, every month. Over time, that recurring fee can exceed the cost of running your own entity with its accountants, payroll provider and legal support.

Run the comparison with your own quotes. Put the EOR fee for your planned team next to the full cost of an entity. Include the cost of people on your side who will manage it.

## Signal: you need to sign contracts locally

Some business models need a local legal presence. You may want to invoice Italian customers in Italy. You may want to bid for tenders. You may want to hold a lease or sign agreements with local partners. An EOR employs your people, but it does not become your company. If your commercial activity needs a local company name, that is a strong reason to open an entity.

## Signal: you want direct control over employment terms

With an EOR, the EOR is the legal employer. You direct the daily work, but the employment contract sits with them. This is fine when you want less admin. It becomes a limit when you want to design your own benefit schemes, run your own HR policies, or shape the employer brand fully under your name.

If candidates ask who they work for and the answer slows hiring of senior people, take note. Some senior hires prefer to join the company itself.

## Signal: the market is no longer a test

An EOR is ideal while you are still learning. Is there demand? Can you hire the right profiles? Does the product fit? If those questions now have clear answers, and the answer is yes, the reason to stay flexible weakens. A proven market justifies the setup effort.

## Signal: you have internal capacity to run it

An entity needs ongoing work. Someone must handle accounting, tax filings, payroll inputs, social security and employment law questions. You can outsource much of it to local providers. Still, you need a person in your company who owns the relationship and checks the output.

If you do not have that capacity yet, staying on an EOR is often cheaper in practice than it looks on paper.

## Signal: investors or partners expect a local company

Sometimes the push comes from outside. A funding round, an acquisition, a large customer or a group restructuring may call for a clean corporate structure in Italy. If that is the case, plan the move before the deadline, not after.

## Signals that you should wait

Do not move just because you feel the EOR is a temporary solution. Stay if any of these is true:

- The team is small and the plan is uncertain.
- You are still testing the market.
- You have no one to own compliance internally.
- Hiring speed matters more than cost right now.

Moving too early adds fixed cost and admin before you have the revenue to carry it.

## How to plan the transition

The move needs preparation. Do these steps in order.

First, set up the entity and the required registrations, with local advisers. Then choose payroll and accounting providers. Agree a transition plan with your EOR, including notice terms in your service agreement. Check how each employee's contract and seniority will carry over, with a local employment lawyer. Then talk to your employees early. Explain what changes and what does not. Pay, role and manager should stay as stable as possible.

Aim for a clean handover date. Avoid a long overlap where some people sit on the EOR and others on your entity, unless you have a reason for it.

## A simple decision test

Ask yourself these questions:

- Will we still be hiring in Italy for the long term?
- Do we need a local legal presence for business reasons?
- Do we have someone to run compliance?
- Is the recurring EOR fee now higher than the cost of doing it ourselves?

If most answers are yes, start planning the entity. If most are no, keep the EOR and review again later.

Many companies move in stages. They start with an EOR, grow the team, then open an entity when the business case is clear. That path keeps risk low and gives you time to learn the local market.
