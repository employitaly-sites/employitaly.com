# Working Time and Leave in Italy: What Foreign Employers Should Expect

> How working hours, holidays and the August slowdown work in Italy, and how to plan for them when you hire your first employee there.

If you hire in Italy, you will meet a different rhythm of work. Hours, leave and the summer break follow rules and habits that may not match your home market. Plan for them early and they cause no trouble.

## Where the rules come from

Working time in Italy is not set by the employer alone. It comes from national law, from the national collective agreement for the sector, and from the individual contract. The collective agreement matters most in daily practice. It sets the weekly hours, the amount of paid leave, extra permits and how overtime is paid.

So the first question is always the same: which collective agreement applies to this role? Your answer decides most of what follows. Do not copy a template from another country.

## Typical working hours

A full-time employee in Italy works a standard week set by law and by the applicable agreement. Many companies run a regular office schedule with a lunch break. Some sectors use shifts or reduced hours as part of the agreement.

A few points to expect:

- Overtime is allowed, but it has limits and it must be paid or compensated as the agreement says.
- Rest periods between working days and a weekly rest day are protected.
- Part-time contracts are common and are regulated in their own way.
- Remote and hybrid work is possible, but it needs a clear written arrangement.

Employees often expect a real separation between work and private time. Late messages and weekend requests are not welcome as a routine. If your team spans time zones, agree the contact window in writing from the start.

## Paid holidays

Every employee earns paid annual leave. The exact amount depends on the contract and the collective agreement. The law sets a floor, and many agreements go above it.

Two habits surprise foreign managers. Leave is taken seriously, and employees are expected to actually use it. Also, a long block of holiday in summer is normal, not a sign of low commitment.

Beyond annual leave, there are other kinds of paid or protected time off:

- Public holidays, which fall on fixed dates across the year.
- Small paid permits that some agreements grant for personal needs.
- Sick leave, with rules on notice and medical certification.
- Parental leave, which is protected and well established.

The employer must record leave properly. Unused leave cannot simply be ignored. It has to be managed and, in some cases, paid out under strict conditions.

## The August shutdown

August is the clearest example of the cultural gap. Many Italian businesses slow down sharply or close for a stretch in mid-summer. Suppliers, clients and public offices may all be hard to reach at the same time.

This affects you in three ways.

**Your own team.** Many employees will ask to take their main holiday in this period. Some companies close the office and require everyone to take leave together. If you do this, say so in advance and count it against annual leave in line with the contract.

**Your clients and partners.** If you sell to Italian customers, expect decisions to stall. Plan launches and renewals around it.

**Your payroll and admin.** Filings and payments still have deadlines. Your payroll provider must plan ahead so nothing is missed while others are away.

## How to plan around it

Ask for the leave calendar early in the year. Agree which weeks are team-wide closure and which are individual choice. Make sure someone can cover urgent matters. Put the rules in the contract or in a short written policy.

A good rule is to avoid surprises. Italian employees respond well to clear expectations set in advance. They respond badly to last-minute changes to leave they already booked.

## Mistakes to avoid

- Applying your home country's leave policy without checking the Italian rules.
- Assuming one agreement fits every role.
- Scheduling key meetings in the summer slowdown.
- Leaving hours and remote work terms vague.
- Forgetting that leave and overtime records must be kept.
