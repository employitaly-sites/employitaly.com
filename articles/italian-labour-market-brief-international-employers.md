# The Italian labour market in brief for international employers

> How the Italian labour market is built, who takes part in it, and why the north and south differ. A short guide for foreign companies planning to hire in Italy.

If you plan to hire in Italy, it helps to know the market you are entering. Italy is not one labour market. It is several, with different structures, different levels of participation and different regional conditions. This article gives you the outline. It does not use statistics, because current figures should always be checked at the source before you decide anything.

## Structure: small firms, strong districts

The Italian economy rests on small and medium-sized companies. Many are family-owned and have been in the same hands for generations. Large employers exist, but they are the exception. For a foreign company, this has a few practical effects.

- Your competitors for talent are often local firms that offer stability and a known name.
- Candidates may be used to a close relationship with the employer. They may expect clear answers on contract type, notice and benefits early in the process.
- Skilled workers cluster around industries, not only around cities.

Manufacturing has a strong place in the economy. Italy is known for machinery, automotive components, fashion, furniture, food and design. These sectors grew in industrial districts, where suppliers, workshops and specialists sit close together. If your business touches one of these fields, you will often find deep technical skills in a specific area. That know-how may not be visible from the national picture.

Services, tourism, finance and technology are also significant. Technology talent is real but more concentrated. It is found mainly in the larger urban areas and around universities and research centres.

## Contracts are set by collective agreements

Most employment in Italy is shaped by national collective agreements. These cover pay scales, working time, leave, notice and many other terms. They differ by sector. The agreement that applies to your hire depends on the work being done, not on your home country or your internal policy.

This matters for planning. Your offer must fit the applicable agreement. Pay is usually set as a gross annual amount, and the employer contributions on top of it are significant. You should budget for the full cost of employment, not only for the salary the candidate sees.

## Participation: who is in the workforce

Participation in the Italian labour market is uneven. In general terms, fewer people take part in paid work than in many other European countries. Three groups stand out.

**Women.** Female participation is lower than male participation, and the gap is wider in some regions than in others. Many women hold advanced degrees. Career paths, childcare and the local job offer all play a part in who stays in work.

**Young people.** Entry into stable work can be slow. Many young graduates begin with fixed-term roles, internships or part-time work. Some move abroad. For an employer, this means there is a pool of educated and motivated candidates who value a clear, stable contract.

**Older workers.** Experienced professionals often hold deep sector knowledge. Retirement rules and career length affect how long people stay in the workforce.

For you as an international employer, the point is simple. Talent is available, but it is not always where the national averages suggest. A role that is hard to fill in one city may be easy to fill in another.

## Regional gaps: north and south

The best known divide in Italy is between the north and the south. The north, especially the industrial areas of the northwest and northeast, tends to have higher employment, higher wages and more international companies. The centre, including Rome, is strong in public administration, services and some technology. The south has lower employment and fewer large employers, though it also has universities, skilled graduates and lower living costs.

What this means in practice:

- In the north, competition for qualified staff is stronger. Candidates may have several offers and expect a fast process.
- In the south, there are often more available candidates for each role. Remote and hybrid work can connect them to employers elsewhere.
- Within each region, there are big differences between a major city and a small town. Do not assume one result for a whole region.
- Cost of living affects salary expectations. A fair offer in Milan may look very different in a smaller southern city.

These gaps are not fixed. Remote work has changed where people can work and who can hire them. Still, local rules, local labour offices and local habits can differ, so it pays to plan by location.

## What this means for your hiring plan

Start with the role, then choose the place. Ask where the skills you need are found, and what the going terms are there. Check which collective agreement applies. Plan for the full employer cost. Expect a hiring culture that values clarity and long-term commitment.

If you do not have a legal entity in Italy, you can still hire. An Employer of Record (EOR) employs the person locally on your behalf, handles payroll, contributions and compliance, and lets you direct the daily work. This is a practical way to test a market before you commit to a subsidiary.
