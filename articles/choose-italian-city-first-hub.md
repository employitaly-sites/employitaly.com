# How to Choose an Italian City for Your First Hub

> A weighted decision framework for picking the Italian city where your first team will sit, based on talent, cost, access, clients and the way you plan to grow.

Choosing a city is one of the first decisions when you hire in Italy. It shapes who you can recruit, what they cost and how fast you can grow. Many companies pick the best-known name and move on. A short scoring exercise gives a better answer.

This article gives you a framework. You set weights, score each candidate city and compare the totals. The method matters more than any single result.

## Step 1: Decide what the hub is for

The right city depends on the role of the team. Write down the answer before you look at any map.

- A sales team needs to be near clients or well connected to them.
- An engineering team needs a deep pool of technical graduates and experienced developers.
- A support or operations team needs language skills and stable, affordable staffing.
- A single senior hire may not need a hub at all. They may work from home, and the city only matters for occasional meetings.

If you cannot say what the hub is for, you are not ready to choose a city.

## Step 2: Set the weights

Use a simple scale. Mark each criterion as high, medium or low importance for your case. Then give high criteria a larger share of the final score. Keep the scale coarse. Fine-grained weights create false precision.

Here are the criteria that matter most, with a typical weight for a first hub.

**Talent pool (high).** Can you find the profiles you need, and can you find more than one? A city with a single suitable candidate is a risk. Look at local universities, existing employers in your field and how many professionals already work in your sector there.

**Total employment cost (high).** Salaries differ between regions. So do rents and the cost of living, which push salary expectations. Check market pay for your exact role in each city. Do not use national averages.

**Transport and access (medium).** How easy is it to reach the city from your head office and from your clients? Consider direct flights, fast trains and travel time for visiting staff. A hub that your own managers struggle to reach will be neglected.

**Client and partner proximity (medium to high).** For commercial roles, being close to customers can matter more than any cost saving. For back-office roles, it matters little.

**Language and international exposure (medium).** Larger business centers tend to have more people with strong English and other languages. Smaller cities may offer loyal staff but a thinner international pool.

**Retention and competition (medium).** A city full of employers in your field gives you choice, but it also means competitors can hire your people. A smaller market may give lower turnover. Decide which risk worries you more.

**Quality of life and relocation appeal (medium).** If you plan to move people from abroad, the city must be attractive to them. Think about housing, schools, safety and daily life. This also affects how easily family members settle.

**Office and space needs (low to medium).** Many teams start in shared space or fully remote. If you do not need an office yet, give this criterion a low weight.

## Step 3: Pick a short list

Choose a small number of candidate cities. Include a large business center, a mid-sized city and, if it fits your sector, a specialized hub known for your industry. Comparing different types of city shows you what you are trading off.

## Step 4: Score and compare

Give each city a score for each criterion. Multiply by the weight. Add up the totals. Then read the result with care.

- If two cities are close, choose the one with the better talent pool.
- If one city wins on cost but loses badly on talent, do not choose it.
- If your top choice depends on one assumption, test that assumption with real conversations before you commit.

Speak to local recruiters, to peers in your industry and to a few candidates. Ask what salaries they expect and what they would need to join a foreign company. This costs little and corrects many wrong assumptions.

## Step 5: Keep the decision reversible

Your first hub does not have to be your last. Hiring through an Employer of Record (EOR) lets you place people in different cities without opening a local entity in each one. You can start with one city, learn from it and add another as the team grows. You can also change your mind if the first choice does not work out.

This lowers the cost of a wrong guess. The decision still deserves care, but it is not permanent.

## Common mistakes

- Choosing the city with the most famous name without checking talent in your field.
- Using national salary data for a local hiring decision.
- Ignoring how your own managers will travel there.
- Weighting everything equally, which hides what really matters.
- Waiting for a perfect answer instead of testing the market.

## A simple rule

Put talent first, cost second and access third. Adjust only if your business has a clear reason to do so. Most first hubs succeed or fail on whether the right people can be found and kept.
