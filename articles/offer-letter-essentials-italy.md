# Offer letter essentials for Italy: what candidates expect to see

> What Italian candidates look for in a job offer: gross pay basis, contract type, collective agreement, role level, benefits and clear next steps.

An offer letter in Italy is read closely. Candidates compare it with the contract they will sign later. They also compare it with what peers in the same sector receive. If your letter is vague, you lose trust early. If it is clear, you look like a serious employer.

This guide lists what Italian candidates expect to see, and what to avoid.

## Start with the contract type

Say plainly whether the role is permanent or fixed-term. Most candidates expect a permanent contract unless there is a clear reason for a fixed term. If the role is fixed-term, state the reason in simple words. If it is part-time, say so and describe the schedule.

Candidates also want to know the start date and the place of work. If the role is remote or hybrid, write down what that means in practice. Do not leave it for later.

## Name the collective agreement

This is the point foreign employers miss most often. In Italy, most employment relationships follow a national collective agreement (CCNL). It sets minimum pay levels, working time rules, leave, notice and many other terms. Candidates know this. Many will ask which one applies before they ask anything else.

Your letter should name the collective agreement that governs the role. It should also state the job level or grade under that agreement. The level drives the minimum pay, so candidates treat it as a core term. A letter that skips it will prompt questions, and some candidates will assume the worst.

The right agreement depends on your sector and the work. Choose it before you write the offer, not after.

## State pay on a gross annual basis

Italian candidates think in gross annual salary. Give the figure in euros, gross, per year. Then say how it is paid.

Many Italian contracts pay the annual amount across more monthly payments than the twelve calendar months. Candidates expect this. Say clearly how many payments the annual figure is split into, so nobody does the wrong mental maths. A candidate who compares your number with a local offer will do it either way. Make it easy.

If you quote a monthly amount, label it gross. Never mix gross and net in the same letter.

## Separate fixed pay from variable pay

If there is a bonus, commission or equity, describe it apart from base salary. Say who decides it, how it is measured and when it is paid. Say whether it is guaranteed or discretionary. Candidates will not count a vague bonus as part of their pay, and they should not have to.

If you grant equity from a foreign parent company, say that the plan rules apply and that the full terms come in a separate document. Do not bury this in a single line.

## List benefits in concrete terms

Candidates expect a short, specific list. Typical items include:

- meal vouchers or a canteen allowance
- supplementary health cover
- a company car or phone, if relevant
- supplementary pension contributions
- training budget
- flexible working arrangements

Only list what you will actually provide. If a benefit comes from the collective agreement, say so. If it is on top of the agreement, say that too. That distinction shows candidates you understand the local system.

## Be clear on working time, leave and notice

Working time, paid leave and notice periods are largely set by law and by the collective agreement. You do not need to restate every rule. You do need to refer to them correctly. A good letter says that these terms follow the applicable agreement and gives the candidate a way to read them.

If you offer more than the agreement requires, say so. That is a selling point, and it deserves a clear line.

Probation is also worth a line. State that a probation period applies, if it does, and that it follows the agreement and the contract.

## Explain who the employer is

Candidates will ask who signs their contract and who pays them. If the employer in Italy is a local entity or a service provider hiring on your behalf, name it. Explain that day-to-day work is directed by your team, while the contract, payroll and social security filings sit with the legal employer.

This matters for trust. A person leaving a stable job wants to know that salary, contributions and the severance entitlement are handled correctly. Say who handles them.

## Keep the language straightforward

Write the letter in English if the team works in English, but offer an Italian version for the contract. Candidates often want to read the binding document in Italian. Make sure the two match on every key term.

Avoid vague phrases such as "competitive package" or "salary to be discussed". Avoid conditions hidden in the last paragraph. Do not make the offer depend on terms that are not yet defined.

## Set out the next steps

End with a short list of what happens next:

- how and by when to accept
- which documents you need from the candidate
- when the formal contract will be sent
- who to contact with questions

Give the candidate a named person and a reasonable time to decide. Italian candidates usually need to handle notice with their current employer, so they value clarity on the start date.

## A short checklist

Before you send an offer to a candidate in Italy, check that it states:

- contract type and start date
- place of work and working pattern
- the collective agreement and the job level
- gross annual pay and how it is split into payments
- variable pay, kept separate from base pay
- benefits you will actually provide
- the legal employer
- next steps and a contact

If any of these is missing, expect questions. Answer them in the letter first.
