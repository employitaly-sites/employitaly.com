# Moving a Team Off an EOR in Italy: Timeline and Lessons

> A practical walk through moving a mid-sized Italian team from an Employer of Record to your own entity: phases, order of work and the lessons that matter.

A mid-sized team in Italy works well under an Employer of Record (EOR). Then the company grows, and the question comes up: should we hire these people ourselves? Many companies reach this point. Few plan it well.

This article walks through a typical exit in the order it happens. It also covers what goes wrong when teams rush.

## Why companies leave an EOR

The reasons are usually simple.

- The team is stable and the hiring plan in Italy is long term.
- Leadership wants direct control over contracts and payroll.
- The EOR fee no longer fits the size of the operation.
- A local entity makes sense for sales, tax or customer reasons.

None of these is a problem. A planned exit is a normal stage of growth. The EOR model is built for it.

## Phase 1: Decide and prepare

Start before you touch any contract. Define what you want at the end. Do you need a new Italian company, or do you already have one? Who will run payroll? Who signs employment documents locally?

Read your EOR service agreement first. Check the notice terms and any handover duties. Ask the provider for a written exit process. A good provider will have one.

At this stage, build a full list of the people involved. For each person, collect the contract type, role, pay structure, benefits, leave balance and the collective agreement that applies. Missing data here causes most delays later.

## Phase 2: Build the legal home

You need an entity that can employ people in Italy. That means incorporation, tax and social security registrations, a bank account, and a payroll set-up. Each step depends on the one before it. This is usually the longest phase, and it is the one companies underestimate.

Do not start the employee transfer until the entity is fully ready. A half-ready entity leaves people without a clear employer, and that creates risk for them and for you.

## Phase 3: Choose the payroll route

Your entity needs a payroll process from the first day. Most companies outsource it to a local provider. Pick one early and run a parallel test using last month's data. Compare the results against what the EOR produced.

Differences will appear. Find out why before go-live, not after the first payslip.

## Phase 4: Talk to the team

People care about three things: will my pay change, will my benefits change, and is my service history safe. Answer these in writing and in plain language.

Hold individual conversations as well as a group meeting. Give people time to read their new documents and ask questions. A transfer that employees understand goes smoothly. One that feels sudden creates doubt, and some of your best people will start to look elsewhere.

Keep the terms at least as good as before. Changing pay, role and employer on the same day is a bad idea.

## Phase 5: Transfer and handover

This is the switch itself. The old employment ends with the EOR, and the new one starts with your entity. The details need care:

- Continuity of service should be agreed and documented.
- Accrued leave and end-of-service pay (TFR) must be settled or carried over in a clear way.
- Final payroll from the EOR and first payroll from your entity must not overlap or leave a gap.
- Benefits and insurance cover must continue without a break.

Ask the EOR to confirm each item in writing. Ask for payroll history, contracts and personnel files in a usable format.

## Phase 6: After the switch

The first payroll cycle under your entity is the real test. Check every payslip. Fix errors fast and explain them to the person affected.

Then close the loop. Confirm that registrations are in order, that the EOR relationship is formally ended, and that nothing is still billed. Review the first period with the team and with HR.

## Lessons that repeat

**Start with data, not law.** Clean employee records save more time than anything else.

**Do not shorten the entity phase.** Every shortcut here returns as a delay later.

**Run payroll in parallel first.** One test cycle finds most errors.

**Communicate early.** People forgive slow timelines. They do not forgive surprises.

**Keep the EOR on side.** A cooperative handover is worth more than a small saving on notice.

**Keep a fallback.** If the entity is late, extend the EOR arrangement for a short time. That costs less than a failed transfer.

## Is leaving always the right move?

Not always. If hiring in Italy is small or uncertain, staying with an EOR can be cheaper and simpler. The right time to leave is when the team is stable, growth is clear and you are ready to run a local employer yourself.

Whatever you decide, plan the exit as a project with an owner, a checklist and a clear order of work. Done this way, the move is quiet and the team barely notices it.
