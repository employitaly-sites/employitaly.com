# Data protection when Italian employee data crosses borders: GDPR basics for HR

> What foreign employers must know about GDPR when HR data on Italian employees moves abroad: roles, legal basis, transfer tools, and a practical checklist.

You hire someone in Italy. Their payroll file, contract and performance notes now exist in Italy. Soon your head office wants a copy. Your HR platform may sit on another continent. At that point GDPR applies, and so does Italian data protection law.

This article covers the basics. It is not legal advice. It will help you ask the right questions.

## Why GDPR applies to you

GDPR protects people in the European Union. It does not matter where your company is based. If you handle personal data of an employee working in Italy, you are in scope. The regulator in Italy is the Garante per la protezione dei dati personali. It is active on employment topics.

Italy also has its own rules on top of GDPR. They cover workplace monitoring, among other things. Local rules often matter more than foreign HR teams expect.

## Who is the controller?

Start with roles. The controller decides why and how personal data is used. The processor acts on the controller's instructions.

In an Employer of Record model, the EOR is the legal employer in Italy. It is usually a controller for the data it needs to run employment: payroll, tax, social security, contracts. Your company may also be a controller for data it uses to direct the work, such as objectives, reviews and access to its systems. In practice the two can be joint controllers for some data.

Do not leave this vague. Write down who decides what. Put it in your service agreement and in the privacy notice the employee receives.

## Pick a legal basis that works

Every use of personal data needs a legal basis. For HR data, the common ones are:

- The employment contract. Payroll, benefits and onboarding fit here.
- A legal obligation. Tax filings and social security reporting fit here.
- Legitimate interest. This can cover some internal reporting, but you must weigh it against the employee's rights.

Be careful with consent. An employee depends on the employer, so consent is rarely seen as freely given. Do not build your HR processes on it.

## Special categories need more care

Some data is more sensitive. Health information, union membership, religion and similar details get stronger protection. Sick leave records and medical certificates are the usual case in HR.

Share these only with people who need them. Your head office rarely needs a diagnosis. It may only need to know that someone is absent.

## Moving data within and outside the EU

Data can move freely inside the European Economic Area. Once it leaves, you need a transfer tool.

The main options are:

- **Adequacy decision.** The European Commission has decided that a country offers equivalent protection. Data can flow to it without extra steps.
- **Standard contractual clauses.** Approved contract terms between the sender and the receiver. This is the most common route for company groups and software vendors.
- **Binding corporate rules.** Internal group rules approved by regulators. They suit large groups and take time to set up.

With standard clauses, you also need to check whether the law in the receiving country undermines the protection. Document that assessment. Add technical safeguards such as encryption where they help.

Remember the vendors. Your payroll tool, cloud storage and support desk may each move data abroad. Each transfer needs its own basis.

## Share less by default

Data minimisation is a core GDPR principle. Ask what your head office really needs.

A finance team usually needs total employment cost, not bank details. A line manager needs working hours and performance data, not tax codes. Aggregated reports solve many requests without exposing individual files.

Set retention periods too. Keep data only as long as there is a reason. Italian law requires some records to be kept for set periods, so align your deletion rules with those duties.

## Monitoring employees

Italian law is strict about tools that can monitor workers at a distance. This includes cameras, location tracking, and software that logs activity. Some tools need a union agreement or authorisation from the labour authorities before you use them.

Foreign companies often roll out global monitoring or security software without checking this. Ask before you deploy anything that tracks behaviour. Employees must also be told clearly what is tracked and why.

## Employee rights

Italian employees can ask what data you hold, ask for corrections, and in some cases ask for deletion or restriction. You must answer without undue delay.

Plan for this before the first request arrives. Know where HR data lives. Know who handles requests. When data sits in several systems across several countries, requests become slow and risky.

## Breaches

If personal data is lost, leaked or accessed without permission, you may have to notify the Garante quickly. You may also have to tell the affected employees. Agree in advance who does what between your company, your EOR and your vendors. A breach is the wrong time to find out nobody owns the process.

## A short checklist

- Map what HR data you hold on Italian employees and where it goes.
- Define controller and processor roles in writing.
- Confirm the legal basis for each use.
- Put a transfer tool in place for every flow outside the EEA.
- Limit access to special category data.
- Check Italian rules before deploying any monitoring tool.
- Give employees a clear privacy notice in Italian.
- Set a process for access requests and breaches.

## How an EOR helps

A good EOR already runs Italian payroll and HR within local rules. It holds the employment records, applies retention rules and handles requests from employees. You still carry responsibility for data you receive and use. The easiest approach is to receive only what you need, through a clear agreement.
