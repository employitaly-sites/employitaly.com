# How to Budget an Italian Headcount for a Full Year

> Plan one Italian hire across a full year. Why monthly payroll and annual cost differ, where the peaks fall, and how to build a budget that holds.

A monthly payroll figure is not a budget. If you hire in Italy and multiply one month of cost by the months in a year, you will get the wrong number. The annual cost is higher, and it does not arrive evenly.

This article shows how to build a full-year budget for one Italian hire. It also shows where the peaks come from, so finance is not surprised.

## Start with the right unit

Most foreign finance teams budget per month. That works for contractors. It does not work for Italian employees.

An Italian employee has a gross pay level, employer social contributions on top, and several deferred or periodic items that build up during the year. Some are paid monthly. Some are paid once. Some are accrued and paid out later.

So budget on an annual basis first. Then spread the result across months in a way that shows the real cash profile.

## The employer contribution layer

Employer social security is the largest add-on to gross pay. One component is the pension, disability and survivors contribution, known as IVS. A secondary source puts the employer share of IVS at 23.81% of taxable pay.

That is one line of the employer contribution stack, not the total. Other contributions apply as well, and the rate depends on the sector, the company size and the contract type. Treat the IVS figure as a floor for your thinking, not as the full on-cost.

Two practical points:

- The percentage applies to taxable pay. If taxable pay rises in a month, the contribution rises with it.
- The figure comes from a secondary source. Confirm the current rate for your case before you lock the budget.

## Why the annual figure is higher than it looks

Monthly cost looks smaller than the annual cost for three reasons.

**Extra payments.** Italian contracts commonly provide additional monthly payments beyond the regular ones. Your national collective agreement sets how many and when. These increase total pay for the year. Contributions follow them.

**Deferred pay.** Italian employees build up a severance entitlement during employment. The employer sets it aside every year and pays it out when the employment ends. It is a real liability from the first month. It does not show up in a simple monthly payroll view.

**Untaken leave.** Paid leave builds up all year. If it is not used, the balance is a cost on the books. Budget for the leave to be taken, and for the cover that may be needed.

## Where the peaks fall

Cash cost is not flat. Expect peaks in the months when extra payments are made. In those months, gross pay is higher than usual, and the employer contributions on that pay are higher too.

The usual pattern is:

1. A regular run rate in most months.
2. A visible jump in the months with an extra payment.
3. A further effect when bonuses or variable pay are paid out.
4. A one-off outflow at the end of employment for the accrued severance amount.

Your collective agreement decides the exact months. Ask for the payment calendar before you build the monthly view.

## A simple way to build the budget

Use a short table with one row per cost item and one column per month.

**Step 1. Gross pay.** Enter the annual gross, including the extra payments, then place each payment in its month.

**Step 2. Employer contributions.** Apply the contribution rates to the taxable pay of each month. Use the IVS share as one line, then add the other lines that apply to your sector and contract.

**Step 3. Severance accrual.** Add a monthly accrual line. This is a non-cash charge in the month, and a cash payment later. Show both views if your board wants both.

**Step 4. Variable pay.** Add bonuses, commissions and allowances in the month they are paid, not the month they are earned.

**Step 5. Other employment costs.** Include insurance, benefits, training, equipment and any mandatory health checks that apply to the role.

**Step 6. Contingency.** Add a buffer for rate changes and for contract changes during the year.

## Two views for two audiences

Finance needs both views, and they answer different questions.

- **Annual cost view.** Total cost of the hire for the year, with accruals. Use it for approvals and for comparing markets.
- **Cash view by month.** Actual outflows. Use it for treasury and for explaining peaks to the CFO.

If you only show the monthly run rate, you will under-report the annual cost. If you only show the annual total, you will miss the months where cash spikes.

## Common mistakes

- Dividing annual cost by the number of months and calling it a run rate.
- Forgetting that contributions are charged on the extra payments as well.
- Leaving out the severance accrual because it is not paid now.
- Using a rate from one sector for a role under a different collective agreement.
- Ignoring that a mid-year start changes which peaks fall in your first year.

## A note on mid-year starts

A hire who starts part-way through the year does not carry the full set of annual items in year one. Extra payments and accruals are usually pro-rated. The first-year cost is lower than the full-year cost, and the second year steps up. Show that step in the plan so the second-year budget is not a surprise.

## What to check before you sign off

Before you approve the headcount budget, confirm four things.

1. Which collective agreement applies to the role, and what it says on extra payments.
2. The current employer contribution rates for the sector and company size.
3. The expected pay calendar, so peak months are placed correctly.
4. Who carries the severance liability in your structure, and how it is shown in your books.

With those four answers, the budget becomes a plan you can defend. Without them, it is a guess.
