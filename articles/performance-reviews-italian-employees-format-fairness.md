# How to Run Performance Reviews for Italian Employees: Format and Fairness

> A practical guide for foreign employers on running fair, well-documented performance reviews for employees in Italy, from format to follow-up.

Performance reviews are normal in Italy. But a review that works in New York or London can cause problems in Milan or Rome. The reasons are legal, cultural and practical. This guide covers the format that works and the fairness points that matter most.

## Why fairness matters more in Italy

Italian employment law protects employees strongly. A review is not only a talk about goals. It can become evidence if a dispute starts later. A poor rating that is vague, undocumented or inconsistent with past feedback is hard to defend.

So treat every review as a record. Write down what was said, on what basis, and what was agreed.

## Check the collective agreement first

Most Italian employees are covered by a national collective agreement (CCNL). It sets job levels, pay components and sometimes rules on bonuses and career progression. Before you design your review process, read the agreement that applies to your employee. Your review must not conflict with it.

In particular, check:

- how job levels and promotions are defined
- whether bonuses or pay steps are tied to set criteria
- any rules on notice, warnings or disciplinary steps

A rating system that ignores these rules can create expectations you cannot meet, or trigger claims you did not plan for.

## Keep ratings separate from discipline

A performance review is a development tool. A disciplinary process is a formal legal procedure with its own steps and rights for the employee. Do not mix them.

If a review shows a serious problem, do not announce a sanction in the meeting. Record the issue, set clear expectations and take advice before any formal step. Skipping this separation is one of the most common mistakes foreign employers make.

## A format that works

Keep the structure simple and repeat it every time.

**1. Agree objectives in writing.** Set them at the start of the period. Make them specific and tied to the role. Both sides should sign off.

**2. Collect input from more than one person.** Ask the direct manager and, where relevant, colleagues the employee works with. This reduces the risk that one person's bias drives the result.

**3. Use a self-assessment.** Let the employee write their own view before the meeting. It makes the talk a conversation, not a verdict.

**4. Use a short, clear rating scale.** Define each level in plain words. Avoid scales where most people end up in the middle with no explanation.

**5. Hold the meeting in private.** Allow enough time. Start with what went well. Then cover gaps with examples.

**6. Send a written summary afterward.** Include ratings, examples, agreed actions and support offered. Invite the employee to add comments.

## Be concrete, not personal

Italian employees often respond badly to vague criticism. They respond well to specific facts. Say "the report was late on these occasions and this is the effect" instead of "you lack commitment."

Keep comments about work and results. Do not comment on personal traits, family situation, health, age, gender, origin or beliefs. Anything that looks like discrimination can create serious risk.

## Treat people consistently

Fairness means similar cases get similar outcomes. Use the same form, the same criteria and the same timing for comparable roles. If you rate two people differently, be able to explain why with facts.

Train managers before they run reviews. Remote managers abroad need the most guidance. They may apply home-country habits, such as blunt feedback or forced ranking, that do not fit local expectations.

## Be careful with data and monitoring

Performance data is personal data. Collect only what you need. Tell employees what you track and why. Be especially careful with tools that monitor activity, such as software that tracks keystrokes, location or screen time. Italian rules on remote monitoring of workers are strict. Do not use such data in a review unless you have confirmed it was collected lawfully.

## Link pay and promotion carefully

If bonuses depend on the review, define the formula in advance. Put it in writing. Do not change the rules after the period ends. If the collective agreement or the contract already gives a pay element, a review cannot simply remove it.

## Language and culture

Offer the review in a language the employee understands well. If the employee works in Italian, give the written summary in Italian too. This avoids misunderstandings and helps if the document is ever needed later.

Expect more discussion than you may be used to. Employees may want to explain context and push back on ratings. That is not defiance. Allow it, note it, and respond calmly.

## After the review

Follow up on what you agreed. Check in regularly, not only at review time. Employees should never be surprised by a rating. If you give feedback throughout the period, the formal review is just a summary.
